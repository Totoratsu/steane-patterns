Syndromes: finding an error without looking at the data

Measuring a qubit destroys what it holds. A syndrome is the way around that: ask a question about several qubits together, never about one, and the answer points at the error.

Key terms

Parity check
Syndrome
Bit flip

Three questions about seven qubits

In Steane Patterns each logical qubit is a triangle. Its seven vertices are the data qubits. Its three faces are parity checks, one on the four vertices around each face. The Z checks (orange) ask about bit flips, the X checks (blue) about phase flips. This chapter follows bit flips; phase flips work the same way with the colours swapped.

The syndrome names the qubit

Write the three check results as three bits, face 1 first. A flip on qubit 4 gives 1 0 1. Read that as a binary number: 101 is 5, so the culprit is qubit 5 − 1 = 4, counting from 0. The same holds for all seven qubits. Each has its own pattern and no two share one.

(3.1)

In words: take the error pattern (one bit per qubit, 1 where a qubit is flipped), multiply by the check matrix and keep each result modulo 2. Column of is the number written in binary, which is why the syndrome can be read as a number.

Intuition

Three yes-or-no answers give outcomes: seven for “this qubit” and one for “nobody”.

Where it stops working

Flip two qubits and the pattern looks exactly like one flip on a third qubit, so the decoder repairs the wrong one. The three flips together form a logical operator, and that is a logical error. A distance-3 code corrects any single flip and nothing more. Larger codes and repeated rounds, the subject of the next chapters, are how that limit is pushed back.

Check yourself

Which faces light up for a flip on qubit 2?

Faces 2 and 3. The pattern 0 1 1 is 3 in binary, and 3 − 1 = 2.

Why can’t the decoder tell two flips from one?

Two columns of added together always give a third column, so the syndrome matches a single flip elsewhere.
